1. Introduction to open science

Open science is essential for increasing discovery, strengthening research transparency, and ensuring that scientific knowledge can be reused and built upon by researchers. Open science (OS) helps research progress faster and further. When publications, data, and software are openly available, other scientists can validate findings, reuse data in new contexts, and build on existing work without unnecessary barriers. This not only improves the reliability of research but also increases its impact across disciplines and borders.

OS provides many benefits to researchers, funders, policymakers, and the broader public.

Things that contradict OS are, for example,

  • requirement to rent or buy articles if one wishes to read them,
  • need for subscription to journals,
  • the limitation of access to code and raw data, e.g. practice of restricting access using the phrasing “upon reasonable request”.

The idea of OS started taking shape in the mid 17th century, when the first academic journal dedicated to publishing science Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society appeared. Until then records of scientific activities were limited to researchers notes.

The term open science grew out of the open access movement and started getting used in the 21st century and its political support started appearing following the Budapest conference organised by the Open Society Foundations in 2002 (https://www.budapestopenaccessinitiative.org/). The view on open science broadened, from having results published in scientific journals, to making all research outcomes available and free to anyone with access to the internet. In 2021, UNESCO published their Recommendation of Open Science, defining Open Science as:

an inclusive construct that combines various movements and practices aiming to make multilingual scientific knowledge openly available, accessible and reusable for everyone, to increase scientific collaborations and sharing of information for the benefits of science and society, and to open the processes of scientific knowledge creation, evaluation and communication to societal actors beyond the traditional scientific community. It comprises all scientific disciplines and aspects of scholarly practices, including basic and applied sciences, natural and social sciences and the humanities, and it builds on the following key pillars: open scientific knowledge, open science infrastructures, science communication, open engagement of societal actors and open dialogue with other knowledge systems.

EC DEFINITION OF OPEN SCIENCE:

Open science is an approach to research based on open cooperative work that emphasizes the sharing of knowledge, results and tools as early and widely as possible. It is mandatory under Horizon Europe, and it operates on the principle of being ‘as open as possible, as closed as necessary’ (e.g. thinking about sensitive data for example).

PRINCIPLES > PILLARS > PRACTICES Open Science is guided by principles such as transparency, accessibility, reproducibility, and collaboration, which aim to make research more open, inclusive, and verifiable.

Under the UNESCO Recommendations on Open Science, these principles apply to the following areas called the Open Science Pillars:

  1. open scientific knowledge,
  2. open science infrastructures,
  3. science communication, open engagement of societal actors and
  4. open dialogue with other knowledge systems.

Scientific publications Peer-reviewed articles, books, and other textual outputs are made free to read and free to reuse. - see section on open access publications for details.

For full transparency of scientific methods, researchers have also been documenting and sharing the process leading up to the final research publication:

Providing open notebooks

Using open computational workflows

Publishing computational workflows,

Publishing experimental protocol
Open data Ensure that data is well-documented, findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable (FAIR) while respecting ethical, legal, and privacy requirements, creating FAIR datasets, and using scientific repositories or general repositories like Zenodo.

The EC describes open data requirements: “Research data is the information (facts or numbers) collected to be examined and considered, and to serve as a basis for reasoning, discussion or calculation. Open access to research data is the right to access and reuse digital research data under the terms and conditions set out in the Grant Agreement. The research data of EU-funded projects must be FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable and Re-usable).”
Open source software, materials and hardware Using and producing open-source software, distributed with the source code and under a licence that allows modification, derivation, and redistribution, for example using GitHub, GitLab repositories, open-source tools.

Open-source materials: reagents, samples etc available via biobanks (e.g. plasmids, cell lines)

Open-source hardware: it is recommended to extend open source definition to hardware
Open peer review Using transparent peer review process leading to transparent quality assurance
Open Educational resources Create and use open training materials and share them under a reusable open license

1.1 Role of Research Infrastructures (RIs) in Open Science

The very databases in which the data are stored and accessed belong under the research infrastructures.

But also the core facilities, synchrotrons and other hardware focused platforms are crucial for enabling Open Science: by providing access to high-quality instruments, standardized protocols, data management platforms, and collaborative spaces, RIs enable researchers to implement open practices effectively. They support the FAIR management of data, the sharing of code and methodologies, and the dissemination of research outputs, while also offering training and guidance to ensure these practices are applied consistently. In this way, RIs act as essential enablers that bridge the gap between Open Science principles and practical implementation.

The term Open Access is also used in the context of the research infrastructures, describing a possibility for external researchers to openly access these infrastructures (data services, experimental services in core facilities or large infrastructures). It has the same spirit of Open Science, but should be distinguished from Open Access to scientific outputs. Open access to RIs is usually regulated in the user access programs and can include some costs. This is covered by other course.

1.2 Benefits of Open Science implementation

The advantage of Open Science is that it addresses key problems of science in general:

  • reproducibility
  • affordability
  • functionality
  • transparency
  • metrics and assessment
  • communication gap and delay between science and society as well as science and policy.

Open Science philosophy reduces inequalities in access to knowledge:

  • Greater visibility means greater reach that creates more networking opportunities enabling new collaborations. Research efficiency increases when research is disseminated on shared platforms, and when shared tools and services are used. This in turn leads to higher scientific impact.
  • Funders, on the other hand, also have the benefit of increased visibility and can reuse funded research. Their funding leads to greater impact and greater Return on Investment (ROI).
  • The General Public and smaller institutes in countries with lower funding profit from faster knowledge transfer and an increased understanding and expertise in terms of research outcomes. Engagement in science and research is promoted as well, increasing societal impact.
  • Organisations and NGOs have an enhanced access to research and profit from more effective advocacy and lobbying.
  • On the level of National Governments, Open Science fosters evidence-informed policy and helps promote Human Rights and democracy.
  • open science broadens access to scientific articles as readers no longer rely on institutional subscriptions to access latest research. Top universities in the UK spend, on average, almost £4 million on subscriptions - it’s easy to imagine not all institutions are able to afford the same level of subscriptions. (See: https://www.timeshighereducation.com/news/top-universities-journal-subscriptions-average-4-million-pounds). Publishers also request higher publication costs, i.e. Article Processing Charges (APCs). The APCs keep increasing with time, which is a huge burden for the research institutes.

Open Science also is helping revolutionise research assessment - putting the focus on all research outputs and not only publications and the journals in which they were published is providing a more open and transparent way to assess research output of a person. This also supports fairness of the system, replacing a single selection step by the journal editors by multiple, open chances to review a person’s work independently. This revolution of the research assessment has been formalized through

  • CoARA, the Coalition for Advancing Research Assessment, is a global network of research organizations that have signed an Agreement on Reforming Research Assessment.
  • DORA, the San Francisco Declaration on Research Assessment

Both initiatives aim to create a more comprehensive and equitable system for evaluating research, researchers, and research organizations.

1.3 Distinction Between Open Science, Open Access, Free Access and where FAIR lays between these

OPEN SCIENCE > OPEN ACCESS > FREE ACCESS — FAIR PRINCIPLES

  • Open Science is a broad term that goes beyond just publications to include data, software, hardware, research infrastructures as well as sharing the methodology and process of research. Additionally, open science is not only about science per se but the whole scholarship, covering all disciplines, and includes open engagement with other actors, collaboration and dialogue.
  • Open Access to scientific outputs ensures that these can be accessed, used, and built upon by the community, ideally under clear licensing and in machine-readable formats where possible.These are open in perpetuity and open licences cannot be revoked. For publications, it includes free access, the author retains copyright, machine readable documents and licences as well as the rights to reuse.
  • Free Access itself does not directly mean that something is Open: when the access is free, anyone can read the content without paying, but to be open, it also needs to be reusable, shareable, and adaptable under clear conditions (usually via an open license).

Open Science and FAIR are often mixed up. Outputs can be FAIR but not necessarily open.

FAIR principles are primarily used for data and software. They ensure that research outputs are Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable in a structured way, often with metadata and standardized formats, making them easier for both humans and machines to use, share, and integrate.

Example: Difference between FAIR and Open

Material can be Open but not FAIR - Freely available to read/use, but poorly structured, hard to find, or not interoperable

Example: A PDF of a research article freely downloadable online, but with no metadata, machine-readable format, or standardized identifiers. Anyone can read it, but software or other researchers cannot easily process or integrate it.

Exercise 3: The “FAIR vs. OPEN” Spectrum Challenge

Duration: 15 minutes

Format: Interactive Poll or Chat Waterfall

Goal: To help participants differentiate between data that is FAIR, Open, both, or neither.

The Setup: The trainer presents four brief scenarios on a slide. Participants must quickly label each scenario using a simple code (e.g., A = FAIR but not Open, B = Open but not FAIR, C = Both, D = Neither).

Scenarios:

  1. The Secret Vault: A highly structured clinical dataset with rich metadata and a persistent DOI, but accessible only to researchers who sign a 10-page privacy agreement.
  2. The Random PDF: A research paper uploaded to a personal blog. It’s free to read, but has no metadata, no DOI, and is not machine-readable.
  3. The Zenodo Gold Standard: A well-documented, machine-readable dataset in a community repository with a CC BY license and a functional DOI.
  4. The Lab Desktop: Raw data stored on a local hard drive in a proprietary format with no documentation.

Activity Flow:

  • 2 min: Show the slide with scenarios.
  • 3 min: Participants submit answers via a poll tool (like Mentimeter) or type them in the chat but wait to hit “Enter” until the trainer says “Go” (Waterfall method).
  • 10 min: Brief discussion on why Scenario 1 (FAIR but not Open) is often necessary for sensitive human data, while Scenario 2 (Open but not FAIR) hinders scientific progress.

1.4 Open Science and intellectual property

OS does not contradict with the protection of intellectual property - patents are granted for inventions not known to the public. Planning beforehand allows researchers to decide which technologies or works will be shared openly and which should be patented. Copyright protects the tangible expression of an idea fixed in the form of a creative work (i.e. in a recording or in print). Ideas cannot be protected by copyright, nor does it protect simple facts. For an output to be protected by copyright, it must be in a tangible format i.e. writing, recording, or discoveries, mathematical methods or computer programs can be copyrighted.

1.5 Challenges in open science

Some of the open challenges still present in relation to Open science are:

  • Most links in publications are no longer available 5 years after publication on average and this impacts the sustainability of the data, even if they were initially open.
  • In the context of the operation of the research infrastructures - It is not always clear who owns the data - sometimes it is facilities, sometimes it is the users of the facilities, sometimes even the funder of the user’s project. In the case of EMBL OS Policy it says “EMBL considers itself as the custodian of the data produced at its facilities” which can be interpreted that either EMBL or external users will make the data publicly available, but it is still not clear what is the role of the infrastructure there and how to implement the policy. Access agreement signed by the infrastructure user usually defines this in more detail.

 

  • At some places new candidates for jobs are still evaluated based on the Impact factor of their publication.
