# Part 2: How Life Sciences contribute to EOSC

!!! question "Exercise: Why is EOSC relevant for Life Sciences?"

    **Instructions**: Run a group discussion on the topic. Invite participants to share their experiences and to say how EOSC would benefit them.

    **Duration**: 10 minutes.

    **Example of points raised by participants in the delivered course**

    - **Diversity and distribution of data.** Life sciences produce large amounts of very diverse data, from simple text files to numerical datasets and images, and many organisations and research teams across Europe work with them. A way of sharing datasets, knowledge and tools is therefore very useful.
    - **Breaking silos.** Sharing across organisations and institutions means not keeping data in-house and letting people communicate and use the same data. Science advances by building on the resources and experience of other people and teams.
    - **Interdisciplinarity.** Life sciences connect different data types of the same research subject – for instance omics data together with imaging data. Physics, and astronomy in particular, may have larger files, but the life sciences have a different need.
    - **Walled gardens by accident.** Experimental data are easily left unshared, so that only a local research group has access to them. Often this happens not because researchers are unwilling but because they do not think of sharing data that are not their primary interest, even though such data may be very useful to somebody they do not know. Mandatory archiving for funded projects is gradually improving the situation.
    - **Standardisation.** When data are widely available in one format, others tend to follow the same format instead of inventing a new one. This “herd mentality” is beneficial because it makes data easier to analyse. EOSC can act as a framework and communicator that shows which practices and standards exist.
    - **Reuse and validation.** Producing data takes a lot of time and resources, so their reuse matters. Access to raw data also allows other researchers to validate an interpretation: in structural biology, for example, reanalysis of deposited data can reveal that a published model has been misinterpreted, usually unintentionally.

## Why is EOSC relevant for the life sciences?

EOSC is highly relevant for the life sciences because the field generates large, complex and highly distributed datasets that need to be shared and reused across infrastructures, countries and disciplines.

Life science researchers commonly face challenges such as fragmented repositories, inconsistent metadata and standards, poor interoperability between tools and workflows, difficulties with provenance and reproducibility, and the legal and ethical complexity of handling sensitive human data. EOSC aims to address these challenges by providing a federated framework that makes life science data, services and workflows more FAIR, interoperable, secure and reusable across Europe.

## EOSC Life Sciences Connect Node

The EOSC Life Sciences Connect Node brings together data, tools and services from leading European life science research infrastructures, creating a unified access point for biomolecular, biomedical, imaging and structural biology resources. The coordinating organisations are ELIXIR, EMBL, Euro-BioImaging and Instruct-ERIC, and the EOSC Federation Memorandum of Understanding was signed in November 2025.

The node aims to support the FAIR integration of diverse life science data types, including omics, imaging, clinical and experimental model data. This enables advanced, cross-disciplinary research and accelerates innovation in areas such as health, disease and biotechnology. The platform also provides tailored virtual research environments, interoperable workflows and secure access mechanisms that comply with the GDPR and uphold ethical standards for sensitive life science data. The node collaborates with other EOSC nodes to create health-related synergies with the European Health Data Space (EHDS), for example with the EOSC Node Germany, the SURF node and the Data Terra node.

The four coordinating organisations have collaborated for a long time and each brings a different strength. ELIXIR contributes a large community and many data resources. EMBL, as a leading European life science organisation, contributes a wealth of multi-omics data as well as databases and services. Euro-BioImaging contributes a strong focus on image-related services, and Instruct-ERIC focuses on services and data related to structural biology.

The Life Science Connect Node is the way in which the life sciences connect, within the new Federation, with the EOSC Association and with EOSC as a whole, which is still growing and emerging. The node itself is organised on several levels:

- a **steering committee** holds the strategic discussions;
- an **operations committee** discusses operations, the more technical aspects, service integration and how services are taken up. It also considers how partner communities are integrated;
- a **partner community** meeting brings the node into contact with other institutions and organisations in the life sciences that are not part of any other node and wish to interact with it; and
- a **technical onboarding** group facilitates the integration of the node’s services, which are still in the build-up phase.

## Contributing to the EOSC federation beside the Nodes

There are opportunities to contribute to shaping the EOSC federation outside the Nodes. Besides taking part in EOSC-run events such as the summer school and the winter school, there are several other formats through which experts can contribute.

Standards and ways of working in a field are rarely imposed from outside. Questions such as why something is done in one way rather than another, or whether different tools or methodologies could be used, are legitimate and are part of how a community works: standardisation happens when a community agrees on a certain standard and on how things are done. EOSC provides a platform on which experts from the community can engage in this process, and it is an invitation from the bottom up. Learners who are experts in their own field can therefore take part in some of these discussions.

### Contribution to EOSC Task Forces

The Task Forces offer staff affiliated with EOSC member organisations the opportunity to contribute actively to the advancement of Open Science. They do so by liaising with EOSC-related Horizon Europe projects, offering feedback on developments, and identifying strategic gaps and areas for investment. Task Forces exist on the following topics:

- EOSC Technical and Semantic Interoperability (EMBL represented)
- FAIR Metrics and Digital Objects
- Health Data (EMBL represented)
- Long-term Data Retention

The Task Forces and expert groups set up by the EOSC Association allow leading experts from across sectors and disciplines to come together, whether they perform research, fund it or approach it from another viewpoint. They reflect on how the sharing and use of data can be made more effective in Europe and easier for researchers. Some Task Forces focus on a single aspect. The Health Data Task Force is an example: health data bring additional layers of complexity, such as data privacy, so that exchanging sensitive human data is considerably more challenging. Other topics, such as training or metadata, are broad, and EOSC addresses them in a community-driven, bottom-up way.

A person who belongs to an organisation that is a member of the EOSC Association can register and become a member of a Task Force; an EMBL employee, for instance, can do so as a member of EMBL.

### Contributing to EOSC Opportunity Area Working Groups

The EOSC Opportunity Area (OA) Expert Groups are a key collaboration mechanism for technical topics within the Horizon Europe co-programmed EOSC Partnership. They are formed through voluntary cooperation among EOSC-related projects and bring together technical experts to drive EOSC development. The OA Expert Groups are a bottom-up initiative of Horizon Europe EOSC-related projects.

The concept emerged during the planning of the first EOSC Winter School and was informed by EOSC Focus interviews for the EOSC Macro-Roadmap, which proposed clustering projects by their contributions to the Action Areas of the Strategic Research and Innovation Agenda (SRIA). Before the 2024 Winter School, EOSC Association Task Forces (2021–2023), initiated by the EOSC-A Board, engaged with projects to share results, provide feedback and identify synergies; this role was continued by the 2024–2025 Task Forces. The process led to six initial Opportunity Areas for the Winter School 2024, which formed the basis of the OA Expert Groups. A seventh group, on research software, was established in November 2024 ahead of the Winter School 2025.

The OA Expert Groups cover topics such as persistent identifiers, metadata and ontologies, and interoperability; research software was the most recent area to be added. They bring together expertise from different EOSC-related projects so that they can communicate about these topics.

### Contributing to funded projects linked to EOSC

Funding opportunities around EOSC have steadily expanded as the initiative has matured into a central pillar of Europe’s research ecosystem. Through successive Horizon Europe work programmes and partnerships, the European Commission supports projects that strengthen the core capabilities of EOSC, such as federated data infrastructures, interoperability frameworks and domain-specific nodes like those in the life sciences.

For researchers and institutions in the life sciences, these opportunities often focus on integrating complex and sensitive data types, ranging from genomics and imaging to clinical datasets, into the EOSC ecosystem in a FAIR and secure manner. Funding calls frequently encourage collaboration between research infrastructures, universities and industry, fostering innovation in areas such as personalised medicine, AI-driven analysis and cross-border health research.

In addition to large collaborative projects, EOSC-related funding also supports the development of virtual research environments, training programmes and services that make data more accessible and reusable. There is a strong emphasis on ethical and legal compliance, so that funded initiatives align with the GDPR and promote responsible data sharing. Together, these funding streams advance the technical foundations of EOSC and empower the life science community to use Open Science for societal impact.

EOSC funding remains active, substantial and continuous, with dedicated calls under the Horizon Europe INFRAEOSC programme as the main entry point. The 2026 topics, for example, focus on advancing the uptake of FAIR data and EOSC by research communities and on developing trusted frameworks for secure and efficient data sharing. These calls are backed by annual budgets in the tens of millions of euros, reflecting a sustained investment in the initiative.

Beyond INFRAEOSC, additional opportunities exist across related clusters and research infrastructure programmes. In particular, “EOSC-aligned” calls within the broader Horizon Europe framework provide complementary funding avenues: INFRADEV (infrastructure development), INFRASERV (user-facing services, including health and life sciences) and INFRATECH (digital tools, AI and data systems). Together, these streams can support a wide range of activities, including life science data platforms, imaging and omics infrastructures, and the development of AI-driven, interoperable data systems, which are often later integrated into EOSC.

EOSC is now established in the funding calls of the European Commission as a permanent function, and its core capabilities have been well funded for years. The calls go beyond the technical foundations of EOSC: they aim at empowering the life science community and at Open Science for societal impact. They are not restricted to members of EOSC or of the EOSC Association and are open to everyone, but applicants must connect their work to the European Open Science Cloud. The work programme itself is a multi-page document in dense language; anyone writing a grant along these lines will find that every word counts, which is why it is worth reading carefully. Calls of this kind usually bring together large consortia across Europe.

!!! example "Example: Publicly funded projects supporting the development of EOSC: STARDAST"

    Project STARDAST is funded under HORIZON-INFRA-2025-01-EOSC-04 (“Data stewards, skills and training for Open Science and FAIR practices”), a Coordination and Support Action under the 2025 INFRAEOSC call.

    The call focuses on building human capacity for EOSC. It aims to strengthen the skills, roles and training programmes that enable researchers and research infrastructures to adopt Open Science and FAIR data practices at scale. More specifically, it supports:

    - the development of data stewardship skills and professional profiles;
    - training programmes and curricula for FAIR data management and Open Science;
    - the coordination of communities and initiatives to harmonise skills frameworks across Europe; and
    - actions that help embed these practices in research workflows and infrastructures.

    The call is part of the broader INFRAEOSC objective to deliver a trusted, interoperable “Web of FAIR data and services”, in which skilled professionals are essential for effective uptake and operation. EOSC-04 funds coordination and training actions to ensure that Europe has the skilled workforce needed to make EOSC and FAIR data practices work in practice.

    **STARDAST in the delivered course**

    The EMBL Data Science Centre found the call very interesting and important, applied, and was successful: the project is coordinated by EMBL and was the only project funded under this topic. It aims at establishing a sustainable, cross-sector ecosystem for professional data stewardship. Data stewardship is relevant for the work of the learners as well, since good data are needed for all kinds of research, including AI. The project was due to start after the summer of 2026.

    The project is cross-sectoral and cross-domain. Its partners cover a good part of Europe and work in fields from the life sciences to particle physics, the humanities and ecology; private partners, such as AstraZeneca and Merck, are also involved. The upskilling of data stewards is not limited to a curriculum: it also includes peer learning, mentorship programmes and train-the-trainer programmes, with Open Science at the centre of all of them, and it is to be expanded across Europe.

    The project also offers opportunities for the wider community. It will use cascading grants to enable at least 25 staff exchanges, which are explicitly open beyond the project partners. Learners may therefore not apply themselves, but they can bring someone from the broader community, or from outside it, to EMBL to host such an exchange. In addition, the project will carry out training pilots and produce training material on data stewardship, which will be openly available. Anyone who wishes to contribute can contact the EMBL colleagues involved in the project.

### Data stewards and AI-ready data

**What is a data steward?** A data steward is a professional who helps to ensure that research and organisational data are properly managed, documented, shared and preserved throughout their lifecycle. When all of this is done, the data can be called AI-ready.

The role is rarely found under this title in research organisations. At EMBL, data stewards are more likely to be found among the EMBL-EBI databases than elsewhere, and there are alternative terms for the same function.

**What does “AI-ready” mean?** There is no real, agreed definition of AI-readiness, and the term remains unclear even in industry. FAIR data are a prerequisite. In addition, the data must come with further metadata and be curated well enough to be meaningful, connected and usable. What is needed also depends on the application: data may have to be prepared differently for a convolutional neural network used for segmentation, for a protein-structure prediction model such as AlphaFold, or for a large language model. What matters is that the data are embedded in a way that the AI in question can understand.

### Cascade funding (Financial Support to Third Parties)

Cascade funding, also called financial support to third parties (FSTP), is becoming more common in upcoming European Commission projects related to EOSC. It is an EU funding mechanism in which:

- the European Commission funds a large project or consortium;
- that project then redistributes part of its budget through smaller open calls; and
- external applicants can apply for smaller, faster grants without submitting a full Horizon Europe proposal.

Cascade funding can be seen as a “second layer” of funding. Its typical characteristics are:

- an easier application process;
- shorter timelines;
- smaller budgets, typically between €50,000 and €300,000; and
- a strong focus on adoption, integration and real use cases.

Cascade grants such as those of the STARDAST project, described above, and of OSCARS are used to fund smaller activities rather than the big consortia. OSCARS, the example worked on in the following exercise, is a further instance of this funding mode.

!!! question "Exercise: OSCARS call: designing a mini proposal"

    **Group exercise**

    Designing a mini proposal, based on the [OSCARS 2nd open call](https://oscars-project.eu/oscars-2nd-open-call-open-science-projects-and-services).

    **Format:** groups of 2–3 people. **Duration:** 20–30 minutes.

    **Instructions**

    Work together to sketch a short proposal concept inspired by the OSCARS cascade funding model, focusing on the integration of a research use case into the EOSC ecosystem.

    **Scenario:** you are applying to the latest OSCARS open call (cascade funding for Open Science projects). Your goal is to propose a small, impactful project (€100,000–€250,000) that:

    - uses or contributes to EOSC;
    - applies the FAIR principles; and
    - demonstrates a clear scientific or societal benefit.

    **Task: develop a one-page mini proposal covering the following points**

    **1. Project idea** (3–4 sentences)

    - What is your research or use case?
    - Which domain does it belong to (for example life sciences, imaging, health, environment)?

    **2. Data and EOSC relevance**

    - What type of data will you use (for example omics, imaging, clinical)?
    - How will your project connect to or use EOSC services?

    **3. FAIR and Open Science aspect**

    - How will your project improve FAIRness (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable)?
    - Will you produce reusable datasets, tools or workflows?

    **4. Impact** (2–3 bullet points)

    - Scientific impact
    - Societal or innovation impact
    - Potential for reuse by others

    **5. Implementation** (brief)

    - What are the key activities (for example data integration, workflow development, validation)?
    - Who are the users or stakeholders?

    **Timing suggestion**

    - 5 minutes: form groups and choose a topic.
    - 15–20 minutes: develop the proposal.

    **Tips**

    - Keep the proposal focused and realistic, with a small project scope.
    - Think cross-disciplinary, for example by combining omics and imaging.
    - Emphasise reuse and EOSC integration.
    - Do not overcomplicate: clarity beats detail.

    **Optional discussion prompts**

    - What made your idea suitable for cascade funding?
    - What would strengthen this proposal in a real call?

    **How the exercise was run in the delivered course**

    - Learners worked in teams of about three people in breakout rooms for roughly 20–25 minutes, and the trainers moved between the rooms to answer questions.
    - Each group received one slide of a shared slide deck containing a table with a box for each of the five points above, in which the group could summarise its discussion.
    - The first task was to agree on a small project idea and to choose a speaker. The purpose was not to evaluate the idea but to practise thinking about how one could contribute through a project, so any idea would do and no time should be spent on choosing it.
    - Groups that struggled were advised to concentrate on the first three points.

    ??? done "Example A of a possible proposal: a platform for the deposition and standardisation of biological imaging data"

        - **Project idea:** a platform for the deposition and standardisation of biological image data. Many groups obtain images in different formats from different microscopes, and the aim is to unify them, ideally in a single standard format.
        - **Data and EOSC relevance:** imaging data (for instance spatial transcriptomics, proteomics and genomics) together with a large amount of metadata: the kind of experiment, the original format, and the format into which the data must be converted. The project would connect to open science services through the sharing of the data.
        - **FAIR aspects:** the resource itself makes the data findable; the data are open and annotated, hence accessible; standardised data and metadata provide interoperability and make it possible to use a single reader; and the same data can be analysed by different groups, which supports reuse.
        - **Implementation:** the group discussed the difficulty of unifying different metadata ontologies. It proposed beginning with a survey of the formats and data types to be handled, followed by their categorisation and a plan for standardisation. A first-tier release would target specific data types and experiment types, with protocols built around them, and would then be expanded. Open questions were whether image source or experiment type is the better basis for categorisation, and whether information embedded in an image file, as geographic information is in a GeoTIFF, counts as metadata or as part of the image itself. A comparison was drawn with the structured deposition forms of the Protein Data Bank, where whatever has been produced has to be matched to the fields of the platform.

    ??? done "Example B of a possible proposal: the impact of pesticides on plant root development and the microbiome"

        - **Project idea:** to study how pesticides influence plant root development and the microbial environment around the roots. The domains are the life sciences and the environmental sciences.
        - **Data and EOSC relevance:** imaging data, obtained by imaging single root cells, and sequencing data from isolated microbes around the roots and in the soil. All of these raw and analysed data should be easy to access through open science services.
        - **FAIR and Open Science aspect:** the raw sequencing and imaging data are made available; imaging protocols and workflows are published so that others can reproduce the work and reuse the data for other purposes.
        - **Impact and implementation:** the results show how pesticides affect root development and the microbiome and can be used by future projects. The research community can use the raw and analysed data, and the isolated microbes could be made available through a German microbial collection (DSMZ).

    **Discussion:** The trainers comment on the presented proposals in order to point out directions to consider when writing a real proposal. Three points are worth remembering.

    - **Existing repositories and standards.** When a proposal plans to share protocols, images or other outputs, reviewers will expect it to say whether repositories and standards for doing so already exist, and how the project relates to them.
    - **Output versus impact.** Describing impact has become more and more important in recent years, and it is often the hardest section to write. Sharing protocols and images is an output. Impact describes what changes as a consequence. As an analogy, switching off the light is an action, whereas the room being dark is the consequence. A project on pesticides would describe impact in terms of what is known about their influence and what then becomes possible, not merely in terms of the data it produces.
    - **Timeline.** A proposal should also say how long the project takes. Cascade grants usually support smaller activities with limited duration, rather than the large consortia of the main calls.

!!! example "Example: A successful OSCARS project: LabID-PROV"

    **LabID-PROV: Tracking and Sharing Data Provenance with RO-Crate in Lab Integrated Data.**

    FAIR data management and the ability to track (meta)data from sample collection to the final computed (or derived) datasets have become increasingly crucial, owing to the growing size and diversity of the data generated across scientific disciplines. To ensure reproducibility, comprehensive provenance metadata must also be available. Lab Integrated Data (LabID) is a web-based integrated platform designed to help individual scientists, research groups and core facilities to manage, annotate and share their experiments, assays, samples and datasets actively and in compliance with the FAIR principles. In LabID, processed data can already be stored and connected to their primary data, associated assays and original samples. However, accurate modelling of both workflows and workflow runs was lacking. The LabID-PROV project extends the LabID data model to include these concepts. It offers a unified application to manage the provenance of derived data independently of the analysis procedure and platform, and thus provides a concrete solution for ensuring the traceability of derived data.

    **LabID in the delivered course (talk by Laurent Thomas, EMBL)**

    The project was presented by the developer whose position it funded. The talk was entitled “Recording data provenance from sample to workflow results”.

    **Why metadata are needed**

    The R in FAIR stands for reusable, and reusable data require metadata. Anyone looking at a dataset needs to know how it was acquired: the protocols used for sample preparation, the specimen (for example a specific transgenic line), the instrument and its settings. Researchers often keep this information in their heads or in a paper lab notebook, but this does not scale: the amount of metadata is large, and a paper notebook cannot reflect the complexity of, and the relations between, a cascade of events. Electronic lab notebooks are more flexible and allow collaboration, but they are not standardised. What is required is a standard platform on which exactly what has been done in the laboratory can be described, without loss of information, and collaboratively.

    **What LabID is**

    LabID (Lab Integrated Data) is an all-in-one FAIR data management platform tailored to the life sciences. It combines an inventory of reagents and equipment, an electronic lab notebook, a laboratory information management system (LIMS) in which the instruments used in an experiment can be referenced, and advanced data management functions. Its idea is to collect data in one place together with all the metadata on their provenance. It has been developed over several years by a team of the EMBL Data Science Centre (the MODIS team, led by Charles Girardot), is available to all EMBL researchers across the EMBL sites using their EMBL credentials, and is open source, so that other life science institutes are interested in using it.

    LabID records every step of an experiment as an entity to which arbitrary metadata can be attached: a specimen, the samples extracted from it, the assay in which they are analysed (for instance imaging or sequencing), the resulting primary or raw data, and finally the derived data produced by a workflow. Protocols used to prepare a sample can be referenced. It supports any type of assay and is useful for multimodal projects, such as spatial omics, in which a sample is examined with different modalities: LabID keeps track of the common sample origin and relates the datasets, even when they were acquired at different sites.

    **Modules**

    - **Inventory (collections):** consumables, equipment, storage and location. Entries can be filtered by any fields, and fields can be defined freely, for example the concentration and application of an oligonucleotide, a location and a contact person.
    - **Biomaterial management:** an inventory of all living material. Inventories are typically handled by a laboratory manager.
    - **Lab notebook:** protocols and experiments, which researchers use on a day-to-day basis. Protocols resemble an office document with attachments and can be exported; they support versioning and collaborative work. An experiment is the execution of a protocol on a specific sample; results such as gel images can be attached, and an experiment can be time-stamped and frozen, which can help to document intellectual property. The owner of any item decides who may view and edit it.
    - **Dataset management:** the management of the data themselves. Samples record how they were generated, refer to protocols, and carry annotations as key–value pairs. Annotations can use predefined values, so that the same information is not entered under different spellings.

    The modules appear in the user interface in the order in which they are used in an experiment. The provenance of a sample can be visualised: for instance, a fly line is the specimen, fly embryos are derived from it, RNA is extracted from the embryos, and sequencing libraries are prepared from the RNA by different methods.

    **Use cases**

    - **Collaboration and day-to-day tracking.** Researchers can build on data acquired years ago, perhaps by somebody else in the laboratory, and still have the information needed to understand and reuse them. The same holds for collaborations with external partners.
    - **Deposition in repositories.** Information can be exported for the deposition of data in research repositories such as the European Nucleotide Archive (ENA) for sequencing data or the BioImage Archive for imaging data. These repositories expect considerable metadata, and LabID makes it easy to compile them.
    - **Computational exploitation.** Machine learning typically needs well-annotated and standardised datasets, and LabID makes the preparation of well-curated datasets easier.

    **The LabID-PROV extension**

    Before the project, LabID documented research data, primarily raw data, and thus covered the experimental provenance. After the acquisition of the data, however, researchers process them with workflows, notebooks or simple scripts and obtain derived data. These derived data could not be registered in the system, so information about how they were generated – the computational provenance – was lost. LabID-PROV closes this gap, and LabID now provides end-to-end provenance from the specimen to the derived data.

    - **Workflows.** LabID interacts with the existing workflow ecosystem. Workflows kept in Git or in Galaxy can be imported. A registered workflow can be published to a workflow repository such as WorkflowHub or exported as an RO-Crate (Research Object Crate), an interoperable format that consists of a directory with a JSON file containing extensive metadata that describe the content of the directory.
    - **Workflow runs.** A workflow run has input and output data. When a run is registered, LabID records which version of the workflow was executed and which data were used and generated, and connects this to the experimental provenance. Runs can be imported directly from Galaxy (on the EMBL instance) and, through a command-line interface, from workflow managers such as Nextflow and Snakemake. They can be exported for publication, again as an RO-Crate, with the experimental provenance added.
    - **Example.** In a registered workflow run for image conversion, an image in a commercial format (a CZI file from a Zeiss microscope) was converted into two OME-Zarr images. The derived images are linked to the raw image and thereby inherit the whole experimental provenance, so that one can tell exactly which sample corresponds to each OME-Zarr image.

    **Funding and the role of EOSC**

    Asked about the funding, the speaker explained that the grant had a cap of roughly 200,000 euros, which was enough to fund the developer for about 20 months. The funding allowed the developer and a colleague to dedicate themselves to the project, whereas the rest of the team is occupied with the support of existing functionality, bug fixing, user assistance and other features, such as the recently added export to S3 storage. Without the funding the development would have taken much longer. The team is now considering building on the results and applying for a further grant.

    **Getting started**

    - The EMBL server is available at labid.embl.org, and a demo server is available at labid-demo.embl.de. Tutorials on the website can be followed with the demo server.
    - The software is open source, and its code is available on GitLab. The team can be contacted through the LabID Mattermost channel or by email.
    - A workshop of one and a half days on the general concepts of LabID and the workflow integration was announced for the end of September at EMBL, in hybrid format; registration is through the website of EMBL BioIT.
