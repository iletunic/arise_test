# 1. Introduction: what is a research infrastructure

## 1.1 What a research infrastructure is, and why Europe shares them

Research infrastructures provide the resources and services that researchers need to carry out their science. Major equipment (a synchrotron, a large electron microscopy facility) comes to mind first, but the term is broader. It also covers knowledge-related facilities such as collections of biocultures or microbial strains, archives, scientific data, computing systems and communication networks.

The European commission defines the following: Research infrastructures are facilities that provide resources and services for the research communities to conduct research and foster innovation in their fields.

These include

- major equipment or sets of instruments
- knowledge-related facilities such as collections,
- archives or scientific data infrastructures
- computing systems
- communication networks

The usage can take shape in the form of physical visit to the facility, sending of samples for analysis or virtual access to digital resources, or even remote operation of the infrastructure. The share of resources reduces the overall costs and maintenance needed for individual labs (so called democratisation of scientific resources).

## 1.3 Need for the Research infrastructures

No scientist can carry out research alone today. Researchers need support, access to instrumentation and access to expertise. To be competitive at the highest level, European researchers need top-end infrastructure, and no single university, lab or even country has access to everything it needs.

Governments cannot fund every piece of equipment in every country, nor give every researcher access to everything. More money alone does not solve this, because no single country can fund 100% of the infrastructure it needs. This is the problem that European governments faced and that the arrangements below address.

To solve this problem, academic community and governments have organized their infrastructure, and this organisation occurs at several levels.

## 1.4 Organisation of the infrastructures

**Local small infrastructures:** infrastructure established and covered by the individual institutes or companies, providing predominantly access to local, internal, users, with some access for external users (core facilities, scientific service platforms and service teams)

**Large scale infrastructures** developed by the capital investment of the governments either of one country or multiple countries which provide access to a wide variety of users and which could not have been created without such large effort. Examples include synchrotron radiation sources, particle accelerators, or large biological research centres. These facilities are often hosted by intergovernmental or national research organisations, such as CERN, EMBL, DESY, or ESA. Many of them are located on single site (DESY) but some are also distrubuted (e.g. EMBL)

**Pan-European networks of smaller infrastructures:** Alongside these large facilities, many smaller research infrastructures—including specialised laboratories, data services, and technology platforms—are organised into pan-European networks through the activities of the European Strategy Forum on Research Infrastructures (ESFRI). These infrastructures often carry a legal framework of ERICs, and they coordinate access, services, and development across multiple countries. Strategic planning for such infrastructures is supported by the ESFRI, which helps EU member states identify priorities and develop new European research infrastructures.

In addition to these formal structures, many research infrastructures also form informal networks and professional associations that facilitate knowledge exchange, collaboration, and community building among facilities and infrastructure staff across Europe. One of the largest associations of this type in life sciences in Europe is Core technologies for life sciences (CTLS), the association of European Core facilities, and in the US it is ABRF ([www.abrf.org/](http://www.abrf.org/)) - Association of Biomolecular Resource Facilities.

Additionally, some groups of academic organisations (universities, institutes) form closed associations of their core facilities which meet and exchange their best practices, e.g. - [Core4Life](https://coreforlife.sites.vib.be/en) in which EMBL, VIB, CRG, MPI-CBG, Curie, Pasteur, FGCZ, and VBCF come together.

Together, these different organisational models allow Europe to operate a diverse and interconnected ecosystem of research infrastructures that support cutting-edge science and technological development.

This coordination ensures more researchers have access to the latest scientific service facilities in Europe, and use their tools and equipment as well as other infrastructure (incl. digital and data).
