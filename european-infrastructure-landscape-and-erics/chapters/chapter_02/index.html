2. Local small infrastructures

2.1 What are core facilities, scientific services and platforms

The term “core facility” is the most established label for centrally managed scientific service units at individual institutes, universities, hospitals or companies. The literature uses it interchangeably with “research core”, scientific platform” or “technological platform” (Bai, Y., & Schonfeld, R. C, 2021; CASRAI Editorial Board, 2026; EU-LIFE, 2016; Oliveira et al., 2025), or in the US “shared research resource”, “service center” or “recharge center”.

The most widely cited definition comes from the US National Institutes of Health, which describes core facilities as centralized shared resources that give investigators access to instruments, technologies, services and expert consultation, typically organised as a discrete unit with dedicated personnel, equipment and space (NIH definition as cited by OHSU, n.d.).

EU-LIFE notes that life-science core facilities, unlike large national or international research infrastructures described in chapters below, are primarily supported by their host institution and are often grouped into small and medium-sized infrastructures with dedicated staff (EU-LIFE, 2025a, 2025b).

Bai, Y., & Schonfeld, R. C. (2021) adds that research cores are shared enablement facilities used across departments, if not across the whole institution, and excludes informal arrangements such as a single lab’s instrument occasionally lent to colleagues. This operational test, namely administration by the institution rather than one principal investigator, service to users across several labs or departments, and at least partial cost recovery, is also stated by CASRAI Editorial Board.

One can argue that this category of research infrastructures also includes data service teams - expert-based units that deliver services around data and analysis rather than around an instrument. The clearest large-scale example is EMBL-EBI, whose stated mission is to provide freely available data and bioinformatics resources and tools as service to the whole scientific community (EMBL-EBI, 2018), through more than 40 data resources and over 150 analysis tools that are free to use worldwide (Cook, Charles 2018).

At institute scale, bioinformatics cores follow the similar logic: they offer consultation, data analysis and data management, and can serve their own researchers as well as outside academic and industry users (University of Surrey, n.d.; LJI, n.d.).

This diversity of views and definitions is reflecting the diversity of the operational, structural and financial set ups used across institutes, technology fields and countries. A 2024 comparative study of European core facilities nonetheless found no clear universally applied definition of core facility or research infrastructure and instead proposed indicators and criteria about its size, operation and funding (Jürgens, A, 2024).

2.2 Who uses core facilities and local service platforms

In Europe, serving users from the home institution as well as users beyond the host institution is part of how these platforms are understood in academic setup.

In Europe, multiple associations of the academic organisations support shared access to their research infrastructures with the notion to improve the scientific progress as such on the pan-European level, e.g.

  • EU-LIFE (The alliance of independent research institutes https://eu-life.eu/ ) partners host 151 such facilities with more than 1,000 dedicated staff who provide a wide spectrum of services to a wide range of users (EU-LIFE, 2025b).
  • Core for Life (Excellence Alliance of Life Science Core Facilities in Europe coreforlife.sites.vib.be/en) was founded in 2012 precisely so that its member institutes share technology and expertise with one another rather than only within their own walls.

The US literature treats the same question mainly as a matter of cost recovery: institutional policies describe a core’s primary purpose as supporting the host’s own researchers while permitting external users, usually at a higher external or for-profit rate, and distinguish open fee-for-service cores from collaborative cores that are not open access and often exist to develop new methods.

Washington State University likewise defines institutional cores by their broad user base across colleges and campuses and by their openness to external users (WSU, n.d.), and tiered pricing, with a lower academic rate and a higher external or for-profit rate, is described as the norm (SFU, n.d. Bai Y 2021).

Taking all these discussions and definition in consideration, we can say that the core facilities and similar institutional service platforms and teams are infrastructures with administration by the institution rather than by one principal investigator, providing service to several labs or departments, and at least to the users from their own organisation, but often external users too, and performing at least partial cost recovery.
