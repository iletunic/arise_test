Introduction to EOSC

Training developers

  • Nina Haberman (EMBL Data science centre Operations manager)
  • Anne-Charlotte Fauvel (EMBL International relations, EU relations lead)
  • Jessica Lindval (SciLifeLab, EOSC Association Board member)
  • Laurent Thomas (EMBL)

Editors: Emily Clark, Tanja Ninkovic, Fellow

Objectives of the course

This course explores why the European Open Science Cloud (EOSC) was created, how it is governed, and how it operates as a federated “system of systems” that connects research data and services across Europe. It also examines how the life sciences contribute to EOSC, identifies practical ways of engaging with it, and gives learners the opportunity to practise designing a small project concept suitable for EOSC-related cascade funding.

The technical implementation details of EOSC services are not covered. Familiarity with FAIR data management is presumed, for example from the earlier ARISE course on this topic.

Learning outcomes based on the Bloom’s taxonomy

By the end of the course, learners should be able to:

  • explain why EOSC was created;
  • place EOSC in the European infrastructure landscape;
  • understand how the life sciences contributed through the Life Science Research Node;
  • identify near-term ways to engage with EOSC;
  • sketch a plausible project idea for a future EOSC or cascade-funding call.

Competencies mapping

This training will contribute to the development of the following RI specific competencies

Knowledge

  • The broad scientific discipline and its ecosystem
  • Familiarity with other disciplines and their relationship to own discipline
  • Changes happening in the wider sector
  • Different types of stakeholders (user communities, policymakers, Board of Governors, etc.), their interests and their sphere of influence
  • Relevant research landscape, including transnational aspects
  • Key relevant funding sources, grant application procedures and the relevant organisational processes
  • Institutional and national financial systems for supporting research
  • Financial/legal landscape as it applies to research infrastructures
  • Open data principles and access
  • FAIR guidelines and principles about data management

Skill

  • Carries out horizon scanning
  • Sees the bigger picture of how own service fits into the organisational and wider landscape
  • Demonstrates how tasks and projects fit in with the bigger picture and broader context
  • Actively and effectively uses networks to the benefit of all parties and in order to facilitate work efforts and gain support
  • Makes use of advocacy and ambassadorship (including lobbying)
  • Aligns the research data management plan and policies to the FAIR principles
  • Aligns the data-infrastructure and tool landscape to the principles
  • Identifies and recognises when problems have the potential to derail an innovative project, and communicates it early
  • Plans business long-term, including forecasting/anticipation of future goals

Attitudes

  • Is agile and responsive to change and new opportunities
  • Recognises what changes are required to keep up to date in the sector or how to react to these changes happening
  • Actively seeks collaborative partners, shares networks and encourages this behaviour in others
  • Engages in continuing professional development to remain up to date on the latest data resources and methods
  • Actively promotes and uses existing standards, ontologies and metadata annotations
  • Facilitates and supports FAIR data
  • Demonstrates curiosity and open mindset for developments outside the immediate field and how they might apply to own service (‘thinking outside of the box’)

Concepts that fellows should adopt

  1. EOSC – the European Open Science Cloud: a federated ecosystem that makes research data and services easier to find, access, share and reuse across countries and disciplines.
  2. Tripartite governance – the strategic steering of EOSC by the European Commission, the EOSC Steering Board and the EOSC Association.
  3. Cascade funding (Financial Support to Third Parties) – a funding mode in which a funded consortium redistributes part of its budget through smaller open calls.
  4. EOSC Life Sciences Connect Node – the node through which the life sciences are connected to the EOSC Federation.

Course timing and structure

The course is designed as a two-hour session that alternates short inputs with interactive elements. Exercises and discussions are marked in the table below; in the chapters they appear in separate boxes.

Time Topic Format
00–10 min Biggest challenges researchers face when collaborating across Europe Exercise (Slido)
10–30 min Why EOSC? How was EOSC created? Input
30–60 min How does EOSC operate? What EOSC is not. Towards the EOSC Federation Input
60–70 min Why is EOSC relevant for the life sciences? Discussion
70–90 min Life Sciences Connect Node; contributing to EOSC through Task Forces, Opportunity Area Working Groups and funded projects, including cascade calls (OSCARS) Input
90–110 min Designing a mini proposal (OSCARS format) Group exercise
110–120 min Closing the course; latest developments – MFF/FP10 uncertainty Input

General principles of course delivery: The course was delivered in an interactive format. Three trainers brought complementary perspectives – the policy side of EOSC, the practical side of participating in EOSC development work, and the view of the EOSC Association – and a software developer contributed a concrete example of an EOSC-funded project. Learners were encouraged to ask questions at any time, and the group exercises took place in breakout rooms. In the delivered course, the presentation of that funded project followed the group exercise on mini proposals.
