Part I: Why EOSC? History and Context

Exercise: What are the biggest challenges researchers face when collaborating and sharing data across Europe?

Instructions

Use Slido to ask the participants: “What are the biggest challenges researchers face when collaborating and sharing data across Europe?” Let participants submit their answers, then discuss several of them and invite the participants’ opinions.

Duration

5–10 minutes.

Points raised by participants in the delivered course

Participants were asked to think about their own experience of working within one institute and of working with other institutes or infrastructures. The answers fell into a few groups:

  • Legal and organisational barriers: data privacy, intellectual property, ownership and patents, and non-scientific restrictions.
  • Technical barriers: the sharing of large amounts of data. The recipient may struggle even to store such data, and transferring it can be slow and costly.
  • Standards: differing data formats and the lack of common data standards.
  • Skills: training on how to share data, who owns data, how to license it, how to use the infrastructure for sharing, and the technology and legal aspects behind it.
  • New developments: artificial intelligence was named as a challenge in its own right.

The data aspect was present in almost every answer. These are the same issues that existed when EOSC was first put in place, and they remain open today.

Why EOSC?

EOSC was created in response to a European research landscape that was rich in infrastructures, data resources and services, but also highly fragmented across countries, disciplines and institutions. Researchers increasingly needed a way to discover, access and reuse data and tools across borders and across scientific domains, rather than working within isolated national or disciplinary silos.

EOSC, the European Open Science Cloud, is a Europe-wide initiative to make research data and digital research services easier to find, access, share and reuse across countries and disciplines. Despite its name, it is not a single “cloud” in the commercial sense. It is better understood as a federated ecosystem that connects existing national and institutional infrastructures, repositories, computing resources, tools and policies into a more coherent environment for researchers.

The problem that EOSC is intended to solve is fragmentation. In Europe, research data and services have often been scattered across different institutions, countries and scientific fields, each with its own standards, access rules and technical systems. This makes collaboration harder, slows down research, and can lead to duplicated effort or to data that cannot easily be reused. EOSC addresses these challenges by promoting interoperability, common standards, trusted access and the FAIR data principles, which require data to be Findable, Accessible, Interoperable and Reusable.

The EOSC story began more than ten years ago, when the idea was driven by the European Commission in 2015 as a European cloud for research. The name itself can be misleading, because it suggests either a commercial cloud provider or a giant European repository in which all research data are stored. Neither was ever the intention. Across Europe, enormous amounts of research data are produced, but they are held in different repositories, managed by different institutions and described using different standards. As a result, researchers frequently struggle to find relevant datasets, to obtain access to them and to combine them with data from other sources. This is particularly true of interdisciplinary research, and it leads to duplication of effort. EOSC was therefore conceived as a response to fragmentation across institutions, countries and data standards.

Its overall vision is to make it easier for researchers to discover, access, share and reuse data and services across Europe. FAIR is a keyword in almost every policy and strategy document behind the initiative, and the aim is to turn it into a reality for all researchers in Europe.

In practical terms, EOSC is meant to help researchers discover relevant datasets and services, combine data from different sources, access computing and analytical tools, and work more openly across borders. More broadly, it supports Open Science in Europe by helping to turn research outputs into shared resources that can accelerate scientific discovery, innovation and evidence-based policy.

For this reason, EOSC was designed not as a single central platform but as a Web of FAIR Data and Services, or system of systems, that connects existing resources through shared standards, interoperability and common governance. Its purpose is to reduce fragmentation, to improve the reproducibility and reusability of research, and to enable more effective multidisciplinary collaboration across Europe.

Why EOSC has become a policy priority

Research investment in Europe is strongly shaped by the European Commission in Brussels, which largely determines where research is heading and how much funding and attention particular topics receive. Over the last two years, EOSC has emerged as more of a priority than before. Two developments explain this.

The first is competitiveness. Europe sees itself as being in a race with other regions of the world and has to compete to remain at the forefront of innovation and technology development. In this context, research data have become a strategic asset, and research is part and parcel of Europe’s competitiveness.

The second is the development of artificial intelligence. There is no uptake of AI without high-quality data; data are the foundation of AI. Europe therefore needs an effective ecosystem in which researchers can easily access, share and reuse research data. EOSC fits this policy narrative: it should make Europe more competitive, allow research to move more quickly and keep Europe in the lead.

How was EOSC created?

EOSC was not created in a single moment. It emerged through a staged European Commission process that began in 2015 (see the European Commission’s Horizon Magazine article on the launch of the Open Science Cloud). In that year the Commission started a long-term alignment and coordination effort to address a major problem in European research: valuable data, tools and services were spread across many separate infrastructures, disciplines and countries, which made cross-border and cross-disciplinary reuse difficult. EOSC was conceived as a response to this fragmentation and as a way of supporting Open Science in Europe.

The idea was formalised in 2016, when the European Commission launched the European Cloud Initiative (ECI), which explicitly proposed the creation of the European Open Science Cloud. The ECI was a broader strategy to strengthen Europe’s data infrastructure, high-performance computing and cloud services for research, industry and public services. Its goal is to build a competitive European data and knowledge economy.

In this phase, EOSC was described as a virtual environment that would allow Europe’s researchers to store, share, manage, analyse and reuse research data across borders and scientific domains. Importantly, the Commission did not intend to build one giant central repository. EOSC was designed as a federated system, which means that it connects and coordinates existing national, disciplinary and institutional infrastructures rather than replacing them.

From 2016 onwards, EOSC was built through a combination of policy development, expert advice and funded pilot projects. The Commission set up expert groups to advise on governance and technical design, and Horizon 2020 funded a series of implementation projects, such as EOSCpilot, EOSC-hub, OpenAIRE-Advance and, later, domain projects such as EOSC-Life, to test how services, repositories, standards and user communities could be connected. This is why EOSC is often described as a “system of systems” or a “Web of FAIR Data and Services” rather than as a standalone platform.

An important public milestone was reached in November 2018, when the first EOSC Portal was launched and the initiative was presented as operationally beginning, although it was still in an early deployment phase. The goal at that point was to move from concept and pilots towards a usable European environment for discovering and accessing research outputs and services. The launch of the portal is often treated as the public launch of EOSC, even though the initiative itself had begun earlier, in policy and preparatory work.

A second major milestone came on 29 July 2020, when the EOSC Association (EOSC-A) was formally established as the legal entity to govern EOSC. This gave EOSC a stable governance structure and created the co-programmed EOSC Partnership between the European Commission and the EOSC Association under Horizon Europe (the tripartite governance structure is described in How does EOSC operate?). The purpose of the Partnership is to coordinate the development and long-term sustainability of EOSC, aligning policies, funding, technical infrastructure and community efforts to create a federated ecosystem for sharing and reusing research data and services.

In summary, EOSC began as a Commission policy initiative in 2015–2016, was prototyped through funded projects and the 2018 portal launch, and became institutionally anchored with the creation of the EOSC Association in 2020.

Discussion: What EOSC is not?

A frequent source of confusion is the expectation that EOSC is a product that can be bought, installed or logged into. Three statements help to correct this.

EOSC is not a single, centralised European data repository. It does not collect all research data in one place, and it was never designed as a giant European super-database. EOSC connects and coordinates many existing repositories, services and infrastructures across Europe through a federated model.

EOSC is not a single cloud provider or one technical platform. EOSC as a whole is much broader than one portal or one service environment. It is a federation of resources, policies, standards and communities.

EOSC is not a replacement for disciplinary or institutional infrastructures. Existing repositories, research infrastructures, national platforms and domain-specific services remain essential. EOSC is intended to make these resources more visible, interoperable and reusable across borders and disciplines, not to replace them.

A single central platform was considered in the early years of the initiative. In the end this idea did not materialise, and the efforts of EOSC were redirected towards connecting existing infrastructures into an interoperable ecosystem (see Towards the EOSC Federation).

How does EOSC operate?

EOSC is governed through what is called Tripartite Governance, also referred to as tripartite collaboration. EOSC is strategically steered by three parties working together:

  • the European Commission (EC);
  • the EOSC Steering Board, which represents EU Member States and Associated Countries; and
  • the EOSC Association (EOSC-A), which acts as Secretariat and relies on the research and infrastructure stakeholder community, whose members and observers take part in the Association.

Although EOSC originated with the European Commission, its day-to-day operation is to a large extent bottom-up. The Commission remains the biggest funder and provides the policy framework in which EOSC operates and evolves. The Member States are represented because a great deal happens at national level in terms of data repositories and research data; with their participation, national policies and investments can be aligned and fitted into the bigger EOSC picture. The EOSC Association represents the voice of the research community and makes sure that EOSC develops in a way that meets researchers’ needs. EOSC is therefore not governed top-down. Its governance model seeks a balance between policy objectives, which may come from Brussels, national priorities set in the capitals, and what the community actually needs.

The European Commission

The European Commission provides the overall European policy framework, links EOSC to the European Research Area (ERA) and funds much of its implementation through programmes such as Horizon Europe. The ERA is the European Union’s policy framework for creating a single, borderless market for research, innovation and technology across Europe. It aims to enable researchers, knowledge, scientific data and technology to move freely between countries and institutions. The Commission is also central in defining the broader political objectives of EOSC, such as Open Science, FAIR data, interoperability and European data sovereignty. In the current federation phase, the Commission also owns and operates the EOSC EU Node, which serves as the first operational reference node in the EOSC Federation.

The EOSC Steering Board

The EOSC Steering Board is the body through which Member States and countries associated with Horizon Europe participate in EOSC governance. It is an expert group of the European Commission made up of national delegates. Its role is to align national policies, investments and priorities with EOSC, so that EOSC is not only an EU-level initiative but is embedded in national research systems. The Steering Board thus brings the governmental and intergovernmental dimension into EOSC.

The EOSC Association

The EOSC Association is the legal entity established to govern EOSC on behalf of the stakeholder community. It was formed in July 2020 and brings together universities, research organisations, e-infrastructures, research infrastructures, funders and service providers as the voice of the community. It represents the community side of EOSC: it mobilises expertise, coordinates stakeholder input, runs task forces, supports implementation work and acts as the formal partner of the European Commission in the EOSC Partnership.

The Association has a broad and active stakeholder base, with 253 organisations involved in total, including 28 mandated organisations, 132 members and 93 observers. This shows that EOSC is shaped through wide participation from across the European research and infrastructure landscape. Members and observers include research-performing organisations, funders, service providers (including research infrastructures) and organisations that combine several of these roles.

The Association is headquartered in Brussels. Because it was only established in 2020, EOSC had no coordinating body in its first years; the Association has now existed for six years. It brings the community together: several times a year, members and observers meet to ensure that the future of EOSC remains aligned with the needs of the community. In this sense, the Association acts as a bridge between policy and practice.

Participation requires the payment of a small annual fee. The membership is diverse and includes universities, funders, service providers, national organisations and research infrastructures. EMBL, for instance, is a member, which enables it to meet the community on a regular basis – people who deal with data from one perspective or another. All countries are represented, and so are all scientific disciplines: EOSC is not only about the life sciences but also covers, for example, the social sciences and physics. The largest share of the members are research-performing organisations.

Example: ARISE2 partner organisations and EOSC

The following ARISE2 partner organisations are connected to EOSC in the ways indicated. Some are involved directly as members or observers, others indirectly through national or European infrastructures.

  • ESRF: member of EOSC
  • CRG: observer
  • UGA: observer of EOSC
  • SciLifeLab: connected to ELIXIR via ELIXIR-SE, and an active partner in the build-up of a Swedish national EOSC Node
  • BIH: Charité is a member of EOSC; BIH has been moved into Charité as a third pillar, and MDC is a privileged partner of BIH
  • CEITEC: involved via ELIXIR
  • VIB
  • Other partner organisations: GSK, Leica, Arinax, Cellsense, Luxendo Bruker, Medical University Vienna, Thermo Fisher, VBCF, Zeiss

EMBL is also a member of the EOSC Association. Partner organisations can be involved even when they are not formally listed as a member or observer: many members are umbrella organisations with their own members, so that participation “trickles down”. There are therefore many ways for a partner organisation to take part in shaping EOSC.

How does EOSC implement its objectives in practice?

EOSC is also implemented and expanded through competitive funding opportunities under the Horizon Europe Research Infrastructures (RI) work programme. Dedicated EOSC-related calls support consortia in developing services, policies, interoperability frameworks, training activities and cross-disciplinary collaborations. These projects help to build EOSC and bring EOSC communities together, either within their own discipline or across disciplines.

The EOSC funding calls enable a wide range of activities across Europe, including the adoption of FAIR data practices, federated access to data and computing resources, interoperability between research infrastructures, secure and trustworthy data sharing, digital research workflows, data stewardship and skills development, and community-driven Open Science services.

Many projects also focus on integrating domain-specific infrastructures into the broader EOSC ecosystem, improving reproducibility and provenance, supporting access to sensitive data, and building thematic or national EOSC nodes.

How the funding works

Horizon Europe is a seven-year programme and the biggest funding programme for research and innovation in the world, with a budget of currently about 100 billion euros. Most of its funding is distributed through open calls for proposals, and ARISE 2 itself is funded by Horizon Europe. The topics of the calls are identified in advance. Any institution can join others to lead a proposal, and the funding can vary from one million euros for a small project to 20 million euros when a whole community is brought together to work on a particular data challenge. The calls are inclusive: an organisation does not have to be a member or observer of the EOSC Association to join or lead a consortium.

There are two ways of being involved in, and benefiting from, EOSC funding:

  • As a consortium partner or leader. The organisation is involved in a project for three to five years and receives direct funding for its work.
  • Through cascade funding. The Commission selects a project and a consortium to address a challenge identified in the call. A large part of the money received by the consortium is intended to be redistributed: the consortium publishes calls of its own for smaller projects, to which the wider community can apply for smaller grants, usually in the range of 50,000 to 200,000 euros, in order to realise their own initiatives.

The topics may be specific challenges concerning life science data, but also training programmes or services that a community needs, so the range is wide. Over its ten years, EOSC has funded more than one hundred collaborative projects of between one and 20 million euros each. The Horizon Europe work programmes show what kinds of large-scale initiatives the Commission, the Member States and the EOSC Association are driving (see Further references).

Example: EOSC-Life: a past project that brought a scientific discipline together

Over the years, the European Commission has funded EOSC projects that help scientific disciplines come together. EOSC-Life is an example from the life sciences. It was a project of roughly 15 to 20 million euros that ran for four to five years and ended a few years ago.

The project brought together the main players of the life science infrastructure ecosystem: those working on mouse models, translational medicine, biobanks, clinical trials, life science data and marine biology, among others. The purpose of this community was to incentivise and support the sharing and reuse of life science data. This meant working on common policies and guidelines, and thinking about which good practices could be exchanged and made available to researchers.

The project website is still live and shows what can be achieved when the life science community comes together.

Towards the EOSC Federation

The EOSC Federation marks a major recent step in the evolution of EOSC from a policy vision into an operational, interconnected European ecosystem. Its rationale is that Europe already has many valuable research data and service infrastructures, but that they remain fragmented across countries and disciplines. The Federation therefore follows a “system of systems” approach: instead of building one central platform, it connects existing national, thematic and institutional resources into an interoperable environment for FAIR data and services. EOSC (the European Open Science Cloud) is the overall vision and ecosystem, while the EOSC Federation is the technical and organisational mechanism that makes this vision work in practice.

Its development is guided by principles such as inclusivity, community participation, trust and governance, flexibility, respect for institutional autonomy and broad stakeholder engagement. These principles make it the framework through which EOSC can become a scalable and sustainable infrastructure for Open Science in Europe.

The Federation was formally launched in November 2025. It is being expanded through successive waves of EOSC Nodes, including national, thematic and e-infrastructure-based nodes; a second wave of 14 new candidate nodes was approved in April 2026.

The Federation represents a new stage in the evolution of EOSC after ten years. A new vision and new leadership have come to EOSC recently, and the idea is to focus the efforts of EOSC on connecting existing infrastructures into an interoperable ecosystem. The initial idea, ten years ago, of building one central platform did not in the end materialise. The process towards the Federation has been bottom-up and community-driven, and it has been under way for about a year and a half. It builds on what already exists; the life science ecosystem described in How the funding works is an important part of this foundation. The Federation, however, covers all disciplines and not only the life sciences.

The vision is ambitious: an environment in which researchers can store, share, access, analyse and reuse research outputs. To achieve it, several key sub-initiatives have been identified that together feed into the Federation. Its approach is both thematic and geographical. The Life Science Connect Node is a life-science-specific node that has emerged from this evolution, whereas other nodes, such as those of Poland, Italy and Slovakia, are interdisciplinary but focus on a particular geographical area. This mix of thematic and geographical nodes is expected to form part of one bigger federation.
